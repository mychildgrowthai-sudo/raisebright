Practical parent guide · Ages 3-12

7 simple communication activities for parents and children

Communication grows through many small exchanges: taking turns, listening for meaning, explaining an idea, asking for clarification, and repairing a misunderstanding. These activities practice those skills without turning family conversation into an interview.

By RaiseBrightPublished August 23, 2026About 9 minutes to read

A useful starting point: these activities are invitations, not tests. Adapt the pace, language, and level of challenge to the child in front of you.

A child may communicate with words, movement, facial expression, drawing, play, silence, or a mixture of these. Give enough time for a response, and avoid filling every pause with another question.

Choose one activity when both adult and child have a little attention available. Five calm minutes can be more useful than a longer conversation that feels forced.

Time5-10 minutes is enough.
MaterialsUse what you already have.
Parent roleGuide calmly, without testing.

Before you begin

  • Get on the same level. When practical, reduce physical distance and competing distractions.
  • Ask fewer questions. Use comments and reflections as well as questions.
  • Wait for the response. Some children need extra processing time before they answer.
  • Do not demand eye contact. Listening and communicating can happen without constant eye contact.
  • Model repair. Adults can say, 'I misunderstood. Let me try again.'

Seven practical activities

1

One-minute listening turns

5 minutes

Take turns choosing a small topic and speaking for up to one minute while the other person listens without interrupting.

How to do it

  1. Choose an easy topic such as a favorite moment or object.
  2. The listener reflects one thing heard.
  3. Switch roles.
Ages 3-5Use 20-second turns with an object to show whose turn it is.
Ages 6-8Use one-minute turns and one follow-up question.
Ages 9-12Summarize the main point before responding.

Parent phrase: 'I heard that the best part was ___. Did I understand?'

2

Describe and draw

5-10 minutes

One person describes a simple picture without showing it while the other person draws from the description.

How to do it

  1. Begin with basic shapes and positions.
  2. The drawer may ask for clarification.
  3. Compare the pictures with humor, not scoring.
Ages 3-5Use two shapes and simple location words.
Ages 6-8Add size, direction, and color details.
Ages 9-12Use a more complex design and discuss which instructions were clearest.

Gentler option: Keep the original picture visible and practice describing one part at a time.

3

Question of the day

3-5 minutes

Choose one question that allows different answers and let every family member answer or pass.

How to do it

  1. Use a concrete or imaginative question.
  2. Let the child ask the next question.
  3. Respond to the answer before moving on.
Ages 3-5What made you smile today?
Ages 6-8What would you invent for our home?
Ages 9-12What is one rule you would keep or change, and why?

Important: Do not use the activity to pressure a child into discussing private or distressing experiences.

4

Story relay

5-8 minutes

Build a story together, taking turns adding one sentence or picture at a time.

How to do it

  1. Agree on a character and place.
  2. Listen to the previous contribution before adding the next.
  3. End after a set number of turns.
Ages 3-5Use one word or picture per turn.
Ages 6-8Use one sentence per turn.
Ages 9-12Add a communication challenge such as dialogue or a change of viewpoint.

Parent phrase: 'I will use your idea and add one part to it.'

5

Clarification game

5 minutes

Practice noticing when information is missing and asking a useful follow-up question.

How to do it

  1. Give an intentionally incomplete direction: 'Put it over there.'
  2. Ask what the listener needs to know.
  3. Try again with a clearer message.
Ages 3-5Use who, what, and where.
Ages 6-8Add when, how, and which one.
Ages 9-12Compare vague, overly detailed, and well-sized instructions.

Parent phrase: 'What information would make that easier to understand?'

6

Repair phrase practice

5 minutes

Choose simple phrases family members can use when a conversation goes wrong.

How to do it

  1. Practice with fictional, low-stakes examples.
  2. Try both the speaker and listener roles.
  3. Choose one phrase that feels natural.
Ages 3-5'Please say it again.'
Ages 6-8'That is not what I meant. Can I try again?'
Ages 9-12'I need a pause, and I can talk after.'

Scope reminder: Practice should never require a child to accept disrespectful or unsafe communication.

7

Solve one small family problem

8-10 minutes

Choose a manageable shared problem, such as misplaced supplies or choosing a game, and work through it together.

How to do it

  1. Let each person describe the problem briefly.
  2. List two or three possible solutions.
  3. Choose one to try and decide when to review it.
Ages 3-5Choose between two solutions.
Ages 6-8Explain one benefit of each option.
Ages 9-12Consider fairness, practicality, and how the plan affects different people.

Parent phrase: 'We may prefer different solutions. Let us find one we can test.'

What can a parent notice?

There is no need to score the activity. Notice small clues that help you choose the right support and level next time.

Turn-takingWhat helps each person wait and stay connected to the conversation?
ClarityDoes the child communicate more easily through words, examples, drawing, or demonstration?
RepairWhich phrase helps after confusion or frustration?
SafetyDoes the child feel able to pass, pause, disagree, or ask for more information?

Good communication is not constant agreement or perfect wording. It includes being heard, checking understanding, respecting limits, and returning to a conversation after a pause when appropriate.

Safety and scope: an adult should choose age-appropriate materials and supervise every activity. These ideas are educational, not an assessment, diagnosis, or treatment. If you have concerns about your child's development, wellbeing, learning, or daily functioning, speak with a qualified professional who can consider the full context.
