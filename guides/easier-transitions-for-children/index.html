Why Transitions Are Hard for Children—and What Helps More Than Repeating Yourself

A practical four-part transition bridge can reduce daily friction without turning every instruction into a negotiation.

By Eriton Kollozi, MD Published September 5, 2026 Approximately 9 minutes

The block tower is almost finished when you say, “Shoes on—we need to leave.”

Nothing happens.

You repeat yourself. Your child asks for one more minute. Then another. By the third request, you are no longer discussing shoes. You are in a struggle over listening, respect and who is in charge.

From the parent’s perspective, the instruction was simple. From the child’s perspective, something absorbing ended abruptly and several new demands arrived at once.

Sometimes children simply resist a boundary. But many difficult transitions are not caused by a child failing to understand the instruction. The adult has communicated the destination; the child must still build the bridge that gets there.

A transition contains more than one task

Moving from play to dressing, from a screen to dinner or from home to school can require a child to:

  • stop an enjoyable activity;
  • shift attention to something less interesting;
  • keep the next instruction in mind;
  • manage disappointment;
  • organize the first action;
  • and begin moving before feeling fully ready.

These demands involve aspects of executive function, including inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility and working memory. These abilities develop throughout childhood; they should not be treated as an on–off switch that a child either possesses or lacks.

This does not mean every protest should be excused or every boundary negotiated. It means that repeating the destination more loudly may add pressure without making the route any clearer.

A routine is the road.
A transition is the bridge.

Family routines can create predictability, reduce the number of decisions required and help children know what happens next.

A systematic review covering 170 studies found that routines were associated with positive outcomes across areas including self-regulation, social-emotional development, academic skills and health. These findings show associations, not a guarantee that one routine will solve every family difficulty.

Another study of children aged 8–9 found that family routines were associated with greater family cohesion and expressiveness and with lower family conflict. Because that study was cross-sectional, it cannot prove that routines caused those outcomes. It does, however, reinforce the value of predictable structure.

Yet having a routine does not automatically make the moment of change easy.

A child may know perfectly well that dinner follows screen time and still struggle when the screen turns off. The road exists. The bridge remains difficult to cross.

The following four-part framework is a practical way to make that bridge clearer. It is not a clinical protocol and does not depend on diagnosing or labelling the child.

1

Preview the ending

A warning is most helpful when it tells the child what will mark the end.

“Soon” is vague. Even “five more minutes” may mean little to a younger child unless time is made visible.

Try connecting the ending to something concrete:

  • “When this song finishes, the screen goes off.”
  • “You can complete this last row of blocks, and then we put on shoes.”
  • “The timer will ring once. Then it is time to come to the table.”

The warning should prepare the child, not reopen the decision. If leaving is necessary, the warning is about when and how the activity ends—not whether it ends.

Repeated warnings can sometimes make the boundary less credible. A clear signal used consistently is usually more useful than five changing deadlines.

2

Help the child close the current activity

Adults often dislike being interrupted halfway through an email, conversation or task. Children may experience the unfinished state even more strongly but lack the words or planning ability to preserve it.

A small closing action can make stopping feel less like losing everything:

  • place the toy car in its “garage”;
  • photograph a block construction;
  • bookmark the page;
  • save the drawing materials in one place;
  • decide where the game will wait until tomorrow.

This is not a reward for complying. It gives the activity an ending and, when appropriate, a believable return point.

The boundary remains firm: the activity stops. But it stops in an organized way rather than disappearing in the middle of the child’s attention.

3

Make the first next step small and visible

“Get ready” sounds like one instruction to an adult. To a child it may contain a chain of hidden tasks: find shoes, choose a jacket, put away a toy, visit the bathroom, locate a bag and walk to the door.

When a child is already disappointed about stopping, the full chain may feel harder than any individual step.

Give the first physical action:

  • “Bring your shoes to the mat.”
  • “Put the tablet on the charger.”
  • “Carry your plate to the sink.”
  • “Open your school bag.”

Once movement begins, give the next step.

This is not doing everything for the child. It temporarily makes the sequence visible. Over time, the steps can be combined or transferred to a picture cue, checklist or routine the child manages independently.

A longitudinal study following 151 mother–child pairs from ages 4 to 6 found associations between positive parenting and the stability of children’s executive-function skills.

Supportive structure is not the opposite of independence; appropriately reduced support can be one way independence develops.

4

Reconnect after movement begins

Parents understandably focus on getting the child to move. Once movement starts, the interaction may still carry the tension of the struggle.

A brief acknowledgment can close the transition:

  • “You stopped before you wanted to. Now we’re moving.”
  • “That was hard to leave, and you brought your shoes.”
  • “You were disappointed, but the screen is off and dinner is next.”

This is neither exaggerated praise nor permission to restart the argument. It communicates that the child’s feeling was noticed while the boundary remained intact.

Connection and firmness can coexist.

Choice is not the same as negotiation

Limited choices can support participation when both options are genuinely acceptable:

  • “Blue jacket or green jacket?”
  • “Walk to the bathroom or hop there?”
  • “Pack the book first or the water bottle first?”

But a false choice creates more conflict:

  • “Do you want to leave now?” when leaving is not optional.
  • “Are you ready for bed?” when bedtime has already arrived.

The destination can remain the adult’s decision. The child may have some ownership over the route.

Listening to a child does not require negotiating every decision. A child can be fully heard without receiving the final vote.

Adjust the bridge by age

Ages 3–5

Use concrete cues, one-step directions and visible endings. A song, object, timer or picture may be more useful than a long verbal reminder.

Ages 6–8

Preview the sequence and offer one meaningful choice within the boundary. Children can begin using short checklists but may still need help starting.

Ages 9–12

Agree in advance on the cue, timing and responsibilities. Give increasing ownership while following through on the agreed boundary.

These are starting points, not rigid rules. Development, context, tiredness, stress and the importance of the activity all affect how a child responds.

Try one transition for seven days

Do not redesign the entire family routine at once. Choose one recurring transition that creates friction—for example, screen to dinner or play to leaving the house.

For seven days:

  1. Use the same clear ending cue.
  2. Give the current activity a closing action.
  3. State only the first visible next step.
  4. Acknowledge the change once movement begins.
  5. Note how many reminders were needed and how long the transition took.

Change only one element at a time. The goal is not a child who cheerfully leaves every enjoyable activity. Adults do not do that either.

Look instead for less confusion, fewer repeated instructions and a quicker return to cooperation after disappointment.

When the difficulty may need more attention

Occasional resistance during transitions is part of ordinary family life.

Consider speaking with a pediatrician or another appropriately qualified professional if transitions regularly cause intense or prolonged distress, interfere substantially with school or family functioning, occur across many settings, or create safety concerns.

A practical routine can support daily life, but it should not replace individualized professional assessment when one is needed.

The boundary can stay firm while the bridge becomes clearer

Parents do not need to eliminate every protest. Children need opportunities to experience limits, disappointment and change.

But a limit does not become stronger simply because it is repeated more loudly.

A clear ending, an organized goodbye, a visible first step and a brief moment of reconnection can turn an instruction into a path the child can actually follow.

The destination stays firm. The bridge becomes usable.
