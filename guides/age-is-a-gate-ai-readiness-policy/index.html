Age Is a Gate, Not an AI Readiness Policy

What New York City’s new moratorium gets right—and what school systems should build next

By Eriton Kollozi, MDApproximately 4 minutes

New York City has drawn a bright line. For the 2026–27 school year, student-facing generative AI is prohibited from 2-K through eighth grade, a one-year moratorium affecting nearly 600,000 students. High school use is limited to vetted, teacher-supervised programs; all high school students receive two AI-literacy modules. The clarity—and the pause—are useful.

But a bright line can cast a shadow. Systems that copy only the grade cutoff will miss the strongest part of New York’s policy: its attention to purpose, dosage, tool design, privacy, adult responsibility, and evidence. Age can open a gate. Alone, it cannot determine whether what happens beyond it is educationally sound.

Age limits do necessary work

An age floor is not an insult to students’ intelligence. It protects children from risks they should not manage alone: persuasive but inaccurate output, opaque data practices, biased answers, and the temptation to trade thinking for the appearance of performance. UNESCO recommends an age limit of 13 for independent conversations with generative AI and pairs that floor with privacy protection, teacher preparation, and age-appropriate pedagogical design.

Age floors are blunt by design. They establish a minimum; they do not measure judgment, subject knowledge, or the capacity to verify a machine’s claims. Two same-age students may approach a tool differently. One student may use AI responsibly in a tightly designed task and surrender the essential thinking in another. Policy must address the relationship among student, task, tool, and supervising adult.

The evidence argues for design, not doctrine

The most instructive study is also one of the least convenient for either camp. In a randomized experiment involving nearly 1,000 high school mathematics students in Turkey, researchers compared classroom resources with two GPT-4-based tools. Students using a largely unrestricted interface performed 48 percent better during supported practice, yet 17 percent worse than the control group on an unassisted exam. A tutor-style interface, designed to provide hints rather than answers, produced larger practice gains without the later penalty.

The peer-reviewed study proves neither that every chatbot weakens learning nor that guardrails solve every problem. It shows that immediate performance can disguise lost learning—and interface design matters.

Evidence on human-AI collaboration points in the same direction. In a preregistered randomized trial—currently available as a preprint—900 tutors working with 1,800 K–12 students received, or did not receive, real-time AI support. With support, topic mastery rose by four percentage points overall and nine points for students assigned to lower-rated tutors. Tutors asked more guiding questions and gave away fewer answers, although some suggestions were not grade-appropriate.

The findings support neither “AI for children” nor “no AI for children” as a general doctrine. AI is more defensible when it strengthens a responsible adult’s pedagogy and preserves the student’s intellectual work; least defensible when fluent output is mistaken for understanding.

The headline is the least transferable part

New York’s moratorium attracts attention because of its scale. Its quieter provisions deserve more. The city’s high school pilots are brief, supervised, and tied to specific instructional purposes. Tools must pass privacy and security review. Necessary assistive technologies and support for multilingual learners remain available. The policy also asks whether students remain the primary thinkers.

Those conditions—not the number on a birthday cake—are the beginnings of a genuine readiness policy. Once student-facing use is permitted for an age group, school leaders should require five affirmative answers:

1. The learning test. What thinking must the student do, and will it remain visible to the teacher? If the tool performs the reasoning being assessed, the task has already failed.

2. The evidence test. Can the student identify uncertainty, trace claims to trustworthy sources, and distinguish plausibility from evidence?

3. The supervision test. Is a trained adult responsible for selecting the tool, framing and observing its use, and stopping it when necessary? The U.S. Department of Education’s principle of “humans in the loop” should be an operational rule, not a slogan.

4. The protection test. Has the system been vetted for privacy, bias, data retention, accessibility, and age-appropriate language? Necessary accommodations must not become collateral damage.

5. The independence test. After the tool is removed, can the student explain the reasoning, reproduce the core skill, or defend the judgment? If not, the system measured assisted output, not learning.

Teach literacy before independence

AI literacy does not require giving younger children unrestricted chatbots. Students can compare human and machine-generated claims, examine why confident language is not evidence, identify synthetic media, and learn what information should never enter a public tool. For older students, autonomy should expand as competence becomes observable—not simply because another birthday has passed.

UNESCO’s student framework describes a progression from understanding to application and creation. Schools can use that logic without turning readiness into a diagnostic label. The question is not whether a child “is ready” in the abstract, but whether a particular use, in a particular context, meets clear conditions and can be withdrawn when it does not.

Pilots should report more than engagement, completion, or time saved. They should measure unassisted learning, error detection, student reasoning, teacher workload, equitable access, and privacy or safety incidents. A tool should earn its classroom role through evidence—and keep earning it.

New York City has made a serious decision in a field too often governed by inevitability and salesmanship. Its one-year pause should not end in a choice between permanent prohibition and broad adoption. It should end with a more precise public standard.

Age should open the gate. Readiness conditions should determine what happens on the other side. The goal is not to make children use AI sooner. It is to ensure that when they do use it, they do not surrender the judgment education exists to form.
