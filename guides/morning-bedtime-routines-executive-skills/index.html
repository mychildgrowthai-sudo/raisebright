When “Get Ready” Is Too Big a Task

Designing morning and bedtime routines that support developing executive skills.

By Eriton Kollozi, MD Published September 7, 2026 Approximately 11 minutes

“Get ready for school” sounds like one instruction. For a child, it may contain many.

  • Stop the current activity.
  • Remember what comes next.
  • Find clothes and belongings.
  • Put the steps in the right order.
  • Estimate how much time remains.
  • Resist distractions.
  • Manage frustration while being hurried.

Adults often perform these operations automatically. Children are still learning to coordinate them. When the morning stalls or bedtime turns into negotiation, adding more words may add more cognitive load without making the next action any clearer.

That does not mean every delayed response is a neurological problem, and it does not mean children never test limits. It means that “won’t” should not be our only explanation when “doesn’t yet know how to organise this task under these conditions” is also possible.

For a broader guide to moving between activities, see Why Transitions Are Hard for Children. Here, the focus is narrower: the two daily routines that often place the greatest pressure on family time—leaving in the morning and settling at bedtime.

Executive function is an explanation—not a diagnosis

The Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University describes executive-function and self-regulation skills as the abilities that help us plan, focus attention, switch gears, manage information and make decisions. These skills continue developing through childhood and adolescence; their expression also varies with the task and the situation.

A difficult routine can involve executive demands, but it can also reflect poor sleep, hunger, anxiety, sensory discomfort, unclear expectations, family stress or a child’s understandable wish to continue a preferred activity. Good support begins with curiosity:

Which part of this routine is difficult for this child, at this moment?

That question is more useful than immediately choosing between two extremes—“the child is defiant” or “the child cannot help it.” Parents can maintain a boundary while making the route to success clearer.

Why broad instructions often produce more prompting

A broad instruction tells the child the destination but not the route. If the child loses track after the first step, the adult repeats the instruction, usually with more urgency. The child then hears more pressure but still lacks a visible next action.

The practical alternative is to move part of the sequence out of memory and into the environment. A short written or picture-based routine can show what happens now and what happens next. Child Mind Institute recommends simple, well-defined steps and easy-to-reference schedules for morning and nighttime routines. Research on visual activity schedules is especially developed in populations such as children with ADHD and autism; findings are promising, but they should not be converted into a guarantee or a universal treatment claim.

If a child repeatedly forgets a sequence, make the sequence visible.

A lower-friction morning design

1

Prepare the environment the night before

Place the school bag, shoes, jacket and other essentials in one consistent location. Lay out clothing when morning choices commonly become a bottleneck. This is not doing everything for the child; it is designing a stable starting point from which independence can grow.

2

Keep the visible sequence short

Use four to six essential steps. Younger children may respond better to pictures; older children can use words, checkboxes or a small whiteboard. A routine that contains every possible detail can become another source of overload.

Example: Bathroom → Get dressed → Breakfast → Teeth → Bag and shoes → Ready at the door.

3

Give one cue, then allow processing time

Rapid-fire reminders can turn guidance into background noise. Give one concrete prompt and pause long enough for the child to act. If nothing happens, identify the obstacle rather than simply increasing volume.

4

Offer limited choice inside a firm boundary

“Blue shirt or green shirt?” supports agency. “What do you want to wear?” may create an unnecessary morning negotiation. The destination remains fixed; the child receives an age-appropriate role in how to get there.

5

Build a small buffer

No visual chart can compensate for a schedule that requires perfect speed every day. A modest buffer protects both the child and the parent from turning every delay into an emergency.

A calmer evening design

Bedtime is not simply the morning routine in reverse. By evening, both children and adults may have less patience, lower energy and more difficulty switching away from rewarding activities.

1

Begin the transition before exhaustion peaks

A routine is harder to learn when it begins only after the child is already overtired. Use a consistent starting cue—such as dimming lights, putting devices away or beginning bath time—rather than relying only on repeated announcements that bedtime is approaching.

2

Use a predictable sequence

Keep the order stable and visible: Bathroom → Teeth → Pajamas → Prepare for tomorrow → Story → Lights out. The chart does not replace the parent. It reduces uncertainty and allows the parent to guide the same sequence more consistently.

3

Treat the screen rule as a sleep-support strategy, not a moral judgment

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends avoiding screens during the hour before bed and keeping devices from interfering with a consistent sleep routine. Reviews have found that screen use is often associated with delayed bedtime and reduced sleep duration.

Newer objective research in 11- to 14-year-olds suggests a more nuanced picture: screen use once the child is in bed—especially interactive use and multitasking—may be more disruptive than every minute of screen exposure before bed.

For families that cannot begin with a perfect 60-minute cutoff, start with the clearest target: no phone, tablet, gaming or short-video scrolling in bed. Then create a repeatable wind-down period with quieter, non-screen activities where possible.

4

End with connection, not only completion

A story, brief conversation or predictable goodnight ritual gives the evening sequence a relational endpoint. The goal is not merely to process a child through tasks; it is to help the household move from activity toward rest.

Support should shrink as skill grows

A visual routine is scaffolding, not a permanent command board. Its purpose is to support performance while a sequence is being learned.

  • Build it with the child where age permits.
  • Model how to use it.
  • Help only with the step that is blocked.
  • Reduce reminders gradually.
  • Review it after a week and remove steps that have become automatic.
  • Change the format if the chart itself creates resistance.

Some children enjoy checking boxes. Others respond better to movable cards, a simple list or a verbal “first–then” cue. Personalization matters more than making the routine visually elaborate.

A seven-day family experiment

Choose one transition—morning or bedtime, not both—and test the following for one week:

  1. Identify the point at which the routine usually breaks down.
  2. Turn the routine into four to six visible steps.
  3. Use the same starting cue each day.
  4. Give one instruction at a time.
  5. Track one outcome: did the number of repeated adult reminders decrease?
  6. Adjust the difficult step with the child at the end of the week.

The aim is not perfect compliance. It is to discover whether a clearer environment allows the child to do more with less adult prompting.

The deeper principle

A routine should not be a daily test of obedience. It should make the desired action easier to understand, begin and complete.

External structure does not weaken independence. When it is matched to the child and gradually reduced, it can give children repeated practice in organising themselves. The most useful question is therefore not, “How do I make my child obey this routine?” but:

What can the environment carry today, while my child is learning to carry more tomorrow?

When to seek additional support

If routine or transition difficulties are persistent, severe, appear across several settings, or are accompanied by significant sleep, anxiety, sensory, developmental or behavioural concerns, consider discussing them with a paediatrician or another appropriately qualified professional.
