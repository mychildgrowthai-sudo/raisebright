Fit Before Formula: How to Adapt Parenting Advice Without Labeling Your Child

Useful parenting advice ought to be based on a solid principle rather than put a family into a set pattern.

By Eriton Kollozi, MDApproximately 8 minutes

On a school morning at 7:20 a parent tells two children the same thing: "Shoes on, please." One of the children requires the reminder, while the other freezes, argues, or breaks down. It is the same words and the same deadline, yet there is a completely different reaction.

It's possible that the first child was occupied with play and therefore forgot all about time. It's also possible that the second child interpreted the countdown as a form of pressure. If the countdown had been said out loud it might have caused one child to move towards the door while making the other one move further away from it.

The rule remains reasonable. The route might have to be altered.

Parenting advice is usually given in a ready-made format—praise the effort, acknowledge the emotions, give the child options, ignore small behaviours, and keep to the boundaries. Each of these suggestions can be helpful. The problem arises when such useful advice turns into a script that has to apply to every child every day. If it doesn't work, parents might blame themselves or the method; too frequently, they blame the child.

What I prefer is this: adhere to the principle but alter the approach. Consider what the advice is intended to teach; after that, examine what this child actually needs in order to put it into use.

Some things should not bend

Just because a parenting approach is personalized doesn't mean that all approaches are equally valid. Certain principles should be maintained: safety, warmth, clear expectations, support that is appropriate for the child's age, the use of positive reinforcement, and discipline that does not involve violence. The World Health Organization's nurturing-care framework defines responsive caregiving as involving the recognition of a child's signals, interpreting them, and then responding in a timely and appropriate manner. Research into parenting programs also supports this general conclusion. A meta-analysis of programs aimed at disruptive behaviour found that positive reinforcement and nonviolent discipline are among the aspects most consistently associated with better outcomes.

That provides parents with a solid foundation rather than a detailed line-by-line script. The National Academies' examination of parenting young children looks at the various practices that generally support children as well as the many factors that influence family life—such as the child, the parent, the household, and the wider social environment. While research can tell us a great deal about what children generally need, it is not capable of giving every parent the exact sentence to use at 7:20 on a Tuesday morning.

A firm boundary can exist even if the method of delivering it changes. The statement "We are leaving at 7:30" sets the boundary. One child might require a five-minute warning, another could find it easier with a series of pictures, a choice as to which shoe to put on first, or with fewer words after the transition has begun. You are not altering the destination; instead, you are assisting each child in finding an approachable way to get there.

Temperament gives clues, not a verdict

From an early age children are different: some respond quickly and strongly, some take longer to become interested, some find it easy to switch their attention while others have difficulty in stopping one activity and starting another. These differences are genuine and they are not flaws, nor are they diagnoses, excuses, or predetermined fate.

A meta-analysis of 84 studies showed that certain aspects of temperament can influence the intensity with which children react to parenting, whether the parenting is supportive or difficult. However, the relationship is not straightforward. The effects differ depending on the trait, the child, the study, and the outcome in question. A recent systematic review of inhibited temperament, parenting, and child anxiety also found only mixed and limited evidence. Therefore, temperament cannot be used to determine which technique will work perfectly; it can only indicate what you should keep a closer eye on.

A label might give a sense of relief since at last there is a word to describe the pattern—sensitive, strong-willed, easy-going, shy, intense. However, labels are tenacious; if someone is described as strong-willed, every disagreement will seem like defiance, and if a person is said to be shy, what starts as a slow warm-up can become a fixed identity. After this has occurred, adults may start to react to the label rather than to the child actually present before them.

Descriptions are more useful since they allow for change. Rather than saying 'She is difficult with transitions', say 'When play ends without warning, she protests and needs a few minutes before she can move'. Instead of describing him as 'lazy with homework', say 'After a long school day, he delays tasks that look large and begins more easily when the first step is clear'. In this way you end up with something specific to work with. A label closes the issue over; a description keeps it open.

When good advice is not helping, ask four questions

The fact that one bad evening has occurred does not show that a parenting principle is incorrect, and it doesn't mean you should say the same things with greater force; instead, you should pause and look for the mismatch.

Actually, what am I aiming to teach?

Is the aim to achieve complete silence or to learn how to disagree without hurting anyone? Is it about finishing the worksheet tonight or about developing a method of starting difficult work tomorrow? It is important to have a clear goal since short-term compliance can appear to be a success even if the child has actually learned very little.

What occurs before the behavior?

It is not enough just to look at the most prominent aspect of the situation. Instead, we should consider what happened just before that. Is the problem more likely to occur when the child is hungry, tired, in a hurry, being watched by other children, or when changing from one activity to another? And at what time does the same request work better? A single instance might deceive us, but a repeated pattern generally gives us more useful information.

What is a minor change that I can make?

Alter one aspect: for example, the timing, the phrasing, the number of options, the size of the first step, the amount of adult assistance, or the level of noise. Leave all the other factors unchanged for a period of time. If you change everything at the same time, you won't be able to tell what made the difference. A break involving movement before doing homework might be helpful for one child whereas another might find it more difficult to get going again. One child may be ready to talk right away after a conflict, while another may need some quiet first. These are situations that should be tested, not new rules to follow.

Does it provide assistance more than once?

Don't just search for a perfect outcome; look at progress instead. Did the child start earlier, require less assistance, communicate more clearly, or apply the skill in a different situation? When it is safe to do so, try the adjustment more than once. If it continues to fail, then alter your best guess. Curiosity is kinder than blame and is much more useful.

Advice meets a real family

Advice won't take root in a home that has no one in it; instead it has to take account of work schedules, culture, money, space, stress, disability, relationships, and the number of adults who can offer help. A review of parenting research carried out across different cultures has shown that warmth, control, monitoring, and discipline can have different meanings and produce different effects depending on the cultural context. A particular piece of advice may be easy to put into practice in a calm home with flexible work hours and a lot of support, but much more difficult to implement in a family that has experienced change, is crowded, is under financial strain, or has heavy caregiving responsibilities.

The fact that this is so doesn't mean that research is without value; it means that the research must be honest with the family. A method which only works under ideal conditions should not be turned into a moral challenge for a family that doesn't have those conditions. Instead, ask yourself: Which aspect of this objective is most important, and what would a practical step towards it look like in this case?

Some problems need more than an adjustment

It isn't always possible to overcome a problem by altering a reminder or by providing a better option. If a child's behaviour is persistent, severe, suddenly changed, dangerous, or is interfering with his or her sleep, eating, learning, friendships, family life, or everyday activities, then you should consult a pediatrician or some other qualified professional. From a distance, developmental, medical, learning, sensory, and mental-health needs may appear as 'noncompliance'. Parents should not be expected to work out these differences by themselves.

The fact that one is asking for help doesn't mean that the parent has failed or that the child has been labeled; it simply shows that the family is now posing a more thoughtful question—what is causing this difficulty and what kind of support does this child really need?

Stick to the goal but alter the course.

There is no need for parents to decide between carrying out research and relying on their own knowledge of their child. Research provides a safe basis to start from, while careful observation shows how it should be put into practice. You should keep firm about the aim of the advice, but at the same time be willing to alter the timing, the language, the kind of support offered, or the setting if the child's reaction indicates that something is not appropriate.

Effective parenting doesn't mean giving the correct reply perfectly; it consists in paying attention, remaining responsible regarding the boundaries, and being open to learning. Keep your aim and then alter your approach so that the child's reaction helps you to decide what to do next.
